Wedding invitation wording in Ireland (with examples)

By Evan, Paper Tide · 16 November 2026

Finding the right words for your wedding invitation can feel oddly daunting. You want it to sound like you, to give guests everything they need, and to hit the right note for your families. The good news: there’s no single correct way. Here’s a simple structure and plenty of examples you can borrow and adapt.

What every wedding invitation should include

Whatever the style, guests need the same essentials. Run through this list before you finalise your wording:

  • Who is inviting: the two of you, your parents, or everyone together.
  • Your names: first names are fine for modern invitations; full names suit something more formal.
  • The request: a line asking guests to celebrate with you.
  • Date and time: written out in full for a formal feel, or in numerals for a modern one.
  • Ceremony venue and, if different, the reception venue.
  • RSVP details: how and by when guests should reply.

Practical extras such as directions, accommodation, a dress code or your wedding website are best kept for a separate details card or your digital RSVP page. It keeps the invitation itself clean and easy to read.

Traditional wording, hosted by parents

Traditionally, the bride’s parents host and their names come first. Formal invitations often use “the honour of your presence” for a church ceremony and “the pleasure of your company” for a civil ceremony or the reception.

Mr and Mrs Seán Murphy
request the honour of your presence
at the marriage of their daughter
Aoife
to
Cian Walsh
at St John’s Church, Waterford
on Saturday, the fourteenth of August
two thousand and twenty-seven
at two o’clock
and afterwards at the reception

Many families now host together, which is lovely when both sides are contributing:

Together with their families
Aoife Murphy and Cian Walsh
invite you to celebrate their marriage
…

Modern wording, from the two of you

If you’re hosting the day yourselves, or you just want something relaxed, speak directly to your guests. Modern wording is shorter, warmer and often a little playful.

Aoife & Cian
are getting married
and would love you to join them
Saturday 14 August 2027
Mount Juliet Estate, Co. Kilkenny
Ceremony at 2pm, followed by dinner and dancing
Please join us for good food, great music
and the start of our forever
Aoife & Cian · 14.08.2027

Church, civil and humanist ceremonies

For a church ceremony, name the church and its town: “at the Church of the Holy Family, Dunmore East”. For a civil or humanist ceremony held at your reception venue, you can simply say “at Mount Juliet Estate, Thomastown, County Kilkenny” and add “Ceremony followed by reception” underneath. If the ceremony and reception are at different places, give both clearly, with times, so nobody ends up at the wrong door.

Adding a line as Gaeilge

A touch of Irish is a beautiful way to make your invitation feel rooted in home. Some couples add one short line; others go fully bilingual. A few popular phrases:

  • Beidh fáilte romhat: you will be welcome.
  • Grá go deo: love forever.
  • Le grá: with love, a sweet sign-off for an RSVP card or thank-you note.

Irish spelling and grammar are easy to get slightly wrong, and fadas matter, so always ask a fluent speaker to check your wording before it goes to print. We’re always happy to set bilingual text carefully in any of our collections.

Children, plus-ones and RSVPs

The kindest way to signal who is invited is by how you address the envelope and invitation, rather than with a blunt line. Name each invited guest, for example “Mary and John” rather than “The Ryan Family”, if children aren’t included. If you’d like to make it clear, a gentle line on the details card works well: “While we love your little ones, our celebration will be adults only.”

For replies, give a firm date and one simple method: “Kindly reply by 1 June” followed by an email address, phone number or the link to your RSVP page. A digital RSVP page makes keeping track of numbers much easier.

Small details that make a difference

  • Write dates in full on formal invitations (“the fourteenth of August”) and keep to one style throughout.
  • Use “o’clock” for formal wording and “2pm” for modern.
  • Read everything aloud. If a line feels stiff to say, it will feel stiff to read.
  • Get a second pair of eyes on names, dates and venues before approving your proof.
